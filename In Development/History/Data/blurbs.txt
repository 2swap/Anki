Hadean
Earth's first eon, named for Hades: a molten surface, constant meteorite bombardment, and the Moon-forming impact. Almost no rocks survive from it, though zircon crystals show liquid water existed surprisingly early.

Archean
Earth's crust cooled and the first continents formed. The earliest confirmed life, single-celled microbes like the cyanobacteria that built stromatolites, appeared in this oxygen-free world.

Proterozoic
The eon of "earlier life": photosynthesis oxygenated the atmosphere, eukaryotic cells arose, and possibly "Snowball Earth" glaciations froze the planet. It ends with the first complex multicellular life, the Ediacaran biota.

Phanerozoic
The eon of "visible life", from the Cambrian explosion to today. It covers every animal with a hard skeleton, the move of life onto land, the dinosaurs, and us.

Eoarchean
The first era of the Archean. The oldest known intact rocks, such as Canada's Acasta Gneiss and Greenland's Isua belt, date from about this time.

Paleoarchean
The era of the oldest widely accepted fossils: roughly 3.5-billion-year-old stromatolites in Western Australia, built by mats of microbes.

Mesoarchean
Stable continental cores (cratons) grew and the first supercontinents may have assembled. Photosynthesizing microbes were spreading but oxygen hadn't yet built up in the air.

Neoarchean
The last Archean era. Oxygen-producing photosynthesis was well established, setting the stage for the Great Oxygenation Event that followed.

Paleoproterozoic
Home of the Great Oxygenation Event, when oxygen first accumulated in the atmosphere, rusting the oceans and causing mass extinction of anaerobic life. The first eukaryotic cells likely appeared.

Mesoproterozoic
Sometimes called the "boring billion" for its stable climate. Sexual reproduction and multicellular algae appear in the fossil record, and the supercontinent Rodinia assembled.

Neoproterozoic
Rodinia broke apart, severe "Snowball Earth" glaciations covered the planet, and the Ediacaran biota, the first large complex organisms, appeared at its end.

Paleozoic
The era of "ancient life": the Cambrian explosion, the first fish, land plants, insects, amphibians and reptiles. It ends with the Permian-Triassic extinction, the worst in Earth's history.

Mesozoic
The "age of reptiles": dinosaurs dominated the land, and the first mammals, birds and flowering plants appeared. The supercontinent Pangaea broke apart, and the era ended with the asteroid impact 66 million years ago.

Cenozoic
The "age of mammals", starting after the dinosaurs' extinction. Mammals and birds diversified to fill the empty niches, grasslands spread, and eventually humans evolved.

Cambrian
Famous for the Cambrian explosion, when most major animal groups appeared rapidly in the fossil record. Trilobites were everywhere, and the Burgess Shale preserves its strange soft-bodied creatures.

Ordovician
Marine life diversified enormously, with the first jawless fish and early land plants. It ended with the second-largest mass extinction, linked to a glaciation.

Silurian
Coral reefs flourished, jawed fish appeared, and vascular plants and arthropods began colonizing the land.

Devonian
The "age of fish": armored placoderms and lobe-finned fish thrived, the first forests grew, and the first four-legged vertebrates (tetrapods) crawled onto land.

Carboniferous
Vast swamp forests formed the coal deposits the period is named for. High oxygen levels allowed giant insects like dragonfly-like Meganeura, and the first reptiles appeared.

Permian
The supercontinent Pangaea assembled and synapsids (ancestors of mammals) dominated the land. It ended with the "Great Dying", which wiped out about 90% of marine species.

Triassic
Life recovered from the Great Dying. The first dinosaurs, pterosaurs and mammals appeared, and the period ended with another mass extinction that cleared the way for dinosaurs.

Jurassic
Giant sauropods and predators like Allosaurus ruled as Pangaea split apart. The first birds, such as Archaeopteryx, evolved from small feathered dinosaurs.

Cretaceous
Flowering plants spread and dinosaurs like T. rex and Triceratops lived at its end. It closed with the Chicxulub asteroid impact, which wiped out the non-avian dinosaurs.

Paleogene
Mammals rapidly diversified after the dinosaurs' extinction, producing early primates, whales and horses. The climate cooled from a hot early Eocene toward the Oligocene.

Neogene
Grasslands spread and the continents took their modern shapes. Apes diversified, and the human lineage split from chimpanzees near its end.

Quaternary
The current period, defined by repeated ice ages. It includes the evolution of the genus Homo and the spread of modern humans across the world.

Paleocene
The first epoch after the dinosaurs' extinction, when mammals began filling empty ecological niches. It ended with a sudden global warming spike, the PETM.

Eocene
The warmest epoch of the Cenozoic, with forests nearly to the poles. Many modern mammal orders appeared, including early horses, whales and primates.

Oligocene
The climate cooled and Antarctica's ice sheets began forming. Grasslands started to spread and large mammals like Paraceratherium roamed.

Miocene
Grasslands and kelp forests spread, and many modern mammal families appeared. Apes were widespread, and the human and chimpanzee lineages likely split near its end.

Pliocene
The climate cooled toward the ice ages and the Isthmus of Panama joined the Americas. Early hominins like Australopithecus walked upright in Africa.

Pleistocene
The epoch of ice ages and megafauna like mammoths and saber-toothed cats. Homo sapiens evolved and spread around the world during it.

Holocene
Our current epoch, starting after the last ice age. It includes the whole history of agriculture, cities and written history.

George Washington
Commander of the Continental Army and the first US president. He set lasting precedents, most famously stepping down after two terms.

John Adams
The second president and first vice president. His term was dominated by the Quasi-War with France and the controversial Alien and Sedition Acts.

Thomas Jefferson
Author of the Declaration of Independence. As president he made the Louisiana Purchase, which doubled the size of the country, and sent Lewis and Clark west.

James Madison
The "Father of the Constitution" and main author of the Bill of Rights. He led the US through the War of 1812, during which the British burned Washington.

James Monroe
Presided over the "Era of Good Feelings" after the War of 1812. His Monroe Doctrine warned European powers against new colonization in the Americas.

John Quincy Adams
Son of John Adams and a skilled diplomat. He won the 1824 election in the House despite losing the popular vote, and later fought slavery as a congressman.

Andrew Jackson
A War of 1812 hero and populist who founded the modern Democratic Party. He killed the Second Bank of the US and signed the Indian Removal Act, leading to the Trail of Tears.

Martin Van Buren
Jackson's vice president and a master party organizer. His term was dominated by the Panic of 1837, a severe economic depression.

William Henry Harrison
Gave the longest inaugural address in history and died of illness 31 days later, the shortest presidency ever.

John Tyler
The first vice president to succeed a president who died, establishing that the VP becomes president in full. He annexed Texas at the end of his term.

James K. Polk
A one-term expansionist who fought the Mexican-American War and acquired California and the Southwest, plus the Oregon Territory by treaty with Britain.

Zachary Taylor
A Mexican-American War general who died in office after 16 months, in the middle of fierce debates over slavery in the new territories.

Millard Fillmore
Took over after Taylor's death and supported the Compromise of 1850, including the Fugitive Slave Act. He sent Commodore Perry to open Japan.

Franklin Pierce
Signed the Kansas-Nebraska Act, which let territories vote on slavery and led to violence in "Bleeding Kansas".

James Buchanan
Failed to stop the growing split over slavery; Southern states began seceding in the final months of his term.

Abraham Lincoln
Led the Union through the Civil War, issued the Emancipation Proclamation, and pushed the 13th Amendment abolishing slavery. He was assassinated days after the war effectively ended.

Andrew Johnson
Took over after Lincoln's assassination and clashed with Congress over Reconstruction. He was the first president to be impeached, and was acquitted by one vote.

Ulysses S. Grant
The Union general who won the Civil War. As president he enforced Reconstruction and fought the Ku Klux Klan, though his administration was marred by scandals.

Rutherford B. Hayes
Won the disputed 1876 election through the Compromise of 1877, which withdrew federal troops from the South and ended Reconstruction.

James A. Garfield
Shot four months into his term by a disappointed office-seeker and died weeks later. His death spurred civil service reform.

Chester A. Arthur
Once a creature of machine politics, he surprised critics by signing the Pendleton Act, which created a merit-based civil service.

Grover Cleveland (1st term)
The first Democrat elected after the Civil War, known for honesty and liberal use of the veto. He lost reelection in 1888 despite winning the popular vote.

Benjamin Harrison
Grandson of William Henry Harrison. He signed the Sherman Antitrust Act and the McKinley Tariff, and six new states joined the Union during his term.

Grover Cleveland (2nd term)
The only president (until Trump) to serve two non-consecutive terms. His second term was consumed by the Panic of 1893, a severe depression.

William McKinley
Led the US through the Spanish-American War, gaining Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines, and annexed Hawaii. He was assassinated early in his second term.

Theodore Roosevelt
Became the youngest president after McKinley's assassination. A progressive trust-buster and conservationist, he started the Panama Canal and won the Nobel Peace Prize.

William Howard Taft
Roosevelt's chosen successor, who broke up more trusts than Roosevelt did but split the Republican Party. He later became Chief Justice, the only person to hold both offices.

Woodrow Wilson
Led the US into World War I and proposed the League of Nations, which the Senate refused to join. His term also saw the Federal Reserve, income tax and women's suffrage.

Warren G. Harding
Promised a "return to normalcy" after WWI. His administration was riddled with scandals like Teapot Dome, and he died in office.

Calvin Coolidge
"Silent Cal" presided over the booming Roaring Twenties with a small-government, pro-business approach.

Herbert Hoover
Took office months before the 1929 stock market crash. His response to the Great Depression was widely seen as inadequate.

Franklin D. Roosevelt
The only president elected four times. He fought the Great Depression with the New Deal and led the US through most of World War II, dying in office weeks before victory in Europe.

Harry S. Truman
Ordered the atomic bombings of Japan to end WWII. He began the Cold War policy of containment with the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan, and fought the Korean War.

Dwight D. Eisenhower
The WWII Supreme Allied Commander. He ended the Korean War, built the Interstate Highway System, and warned of the "military-industrial complex".

John F. Kennedy
Faced down the Soviets in the Cuban Missile Crisis and set the goal of landing on the Moon. He was assassinated in Dallas in 1963.

Lyndon B. Johnson
Passed the Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, Medicare and Medicaid as part of his "Great Society", but escalated the unpopular Vietnam War.

Richard Nixon
Opened relations with China and pursued détente with the USSR. He resigned over the Watergate scandal, the only president ever to do so.

Gerald Ford
The only president never elected president or vice president. He pardoned Nixon and oversaw the end of the Vietnam War.

Jimmy Carter
Brokered the Camp David Accords between Egypt and Israel. His term was hurt by inflation, an energy crisis and the Iran hostage crisis.

Ronald Reagan
A former actor whose "Reaganomics" cut taxes and regulation. He built up the military and negotiated arms reductions with Gorbachev as the Cold War wound down.

George H.W. Bush
President as the Berlin Wall fell and the Soviet Union dissolved. He led a coalition to expel Iraq from Kuwait in the Gulf War.

Bill Clinton
Presided over a long economic boom and balanced budgets, and signed NAFTA. He was impeached over the Lewinsky scandal and acquitted.

George W. Bush
His presidency was defined by the September 11 attacks, which led to the wars in Afghanistan and Iraq. It ended in the 2008 financial crisis.

Barack Obama
The first Black president. He passed the Affordable Care Act, managed the recovery from the Great Recession, and ordered the raid that killed Osama bin Laden.

Donald Trump
A businessman and TV personality who became president without prior political or military service. He cut taxes, reshaped trade policy, and was impeached twice and acquitted both times.

Joe Biden
Vice president under Obama. He took office during the COVID-19 pandemic, oversaw the withdrawal from Afghanistan, and passed major infrastructure and climate bills.

Civil War
Fought between the Union and eleven Confederate states that seceded over slavery. It was the deadliest war in US history, with about 620,000 or more deaths, and ended slavery.

American Revolutionary War
The thirteen colonies fought for independence from Britain, with crucial help from France. It ended with the Treaty of Paris recognizing the United States.

World War I
The "Great War" between the Allies and the Central Powers, with around 20 million dead. The US joined in 1917, helping tip the balance toward the Allies.

World War II
The deadliest conflict in history, with over 70 million dead, including the Holocaust. The US entered after Pearl Harbor in 1941, and the war ended with the atomic bombings of Japan.

Korean War
North Korea, backed by China and the USSR, invaded South Korea, which was defended by a US-led UN force. It ended in an armistice near the original border, and no peace treaty has ever been signed.

Vietnam War
The US backed South Vietnam against the communist North and the Viet Cong. Deeply divisive at home, it ended with the fall of Saigon and a unified communist Vietnam.

Gulf War
A US-led coalition expelled Iraq from Kuwait after Saddam Hussein's invasion. The ground war lasted only about 100 hours.

War in Afghanistan
Launched after the September 11 attacks to topple the Taliban and destroy al-Qaeda. The longest war in US history, it ended with a chaotic withdrawal and the Taliban's return to power.

Iraq War
The US invaded Iraq and overthrew Saddam Hussein over weapons of mass destruction that were never found. A long insurgency and sectarian violence followed.

夏朝 Xià Cháo
The first dynasty in traditional Chinese history, founded by Yu the Great, who was said to have tamed the floods. No writing from it survives, so its existence is still debated.

商朝 Shāng Cháo
The first Chinese dynasty confirmed by writing. Oracle bones from it carry the earliest Chinese characters, and it was known for its bronze casting.

周朝 Zhōu Cháo
The longest-lasting Chinese dynasty. It introduced the idea of the "Mandate of Heaven", and its chaotic later centuries (the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods) produced Confucius and Laozi.

秦朝 Qín Cháo
Qin Shi Huang unified China for the first time, standardizing writing, currency and measurements. The dynasty was short and brutal, but it gave China its name and the Terracotta Army.

汉朝 Hàn Cháo
A golden age that defined Chinese identity; the Han Chinese are named after it. It opened the Silk Road, made Confucianism the state ideology, and invented paper.

魏晋南北朝 Wèi Jìn Nánběi Cháo
Nearly four centuries of division after the Han, including the Three Kingdoms era. Buddhism spread widely through China during this time.

隋朝 Suí Cháo
A short dynasty that reunified China and built the Grand Canal. Its overreach, especially the failed wars in Korea, led to its quick collapse.

唐朝 Táng Cháo
Often considered the height of Chinese civilization, with a cosmopolitan capital at Chang'an. It was a golden age of poetry, and woodblock printing developed during it.

五代十国 Wǔdài Shígúo
The "Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms": a half-century of fragmentation after the fall of the Tang, with short-lived regimes in the north and several kingdoms in the south.

宋朝 Sòng Cháo
A time of economic and technological flourishing: movable type, gunpowder weapons, the magnetic compass and paper money. It fell to the Mongols.

元朝 Yuán Cháo
The Mongol dynasty founded by Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan. Marco Polo reportedly visited its court.

明朝 Míng Cháo
Restored Han Chinese rule after the Mongols. It built most of today's Great Wall and the Forbidden City, and sent Zheng He's treasure fleets as far as Africa.

清朝 Qīng Cháo
China's last imperial dynasty, ruled by the Manchus. It reached its greatest size in the 1700s, then declined through the Opium Wars and rebellions, and fell in the 1911 revolution.

Assyrian Empire
The Neo-Assyrian Empire, from its capital at Nineveh in Mesopotamia, was the ancient world's first great military empire. It used iron weapons, siege warfare and mass deportations.

Achaemenid Empire
The first Persian Empire, founded by Cyrus the Great. It was the largest empire the world had yet seen, known for its Royal Road and tolerance of local customs, and it fell to Alexander.

Macedonian Empire
Alexander the Great conquered from Greece to India, spreading Greek culture across the Near East. After his death in 323 BC his generals split it into Hellenistic kingdoms, the last of which (Ptolemaic Egypt) fell in 30 BC.

Roman Empire
Began when Augustus became the first emperor. At its peak it ruled the whole Mediterranean, and it shaped Western law, language and religion. The western half fell in 476.

Byzantine Empire
The eastern continuation of the Roman Empire, ruled from Constantinople. It preserved Greek and Roman learning for a thousand years until the Ottomans conquered it in 1453.

Islamic Caliphate
From the Rashidun caliphs through the Umayyads and Abbasids, the caliphate spread Islam from Spain to Central Asia. Baghdad became a center of the Islamic Golden Age until the Mongols sacked it.

Mongol Empire
Founded by Genghis Khan, it became the largest contiguous land empire in history. It connected East and West along the Silk Road, and it may also have helped spread the Black Death.

Mughal Empire
A Muslim dynasty descended from Timur and Genghis Khan that ruled most of the Indian subcontinent. It built the Taj Mahal, and was ended by the British after the 1857 rebellion.

British Empire
At its height it covered about a quarter of the world's land and people: "the empire on which the sun never sets". It was largely dismantled after WWII, with Hong Kong's handover in 1997 often seen as its end.

Soviet Union
The world's first communist state, formed after the Russian Revolution. It was decisive in defeating Nazi Germany, then led the Eastern Bloc in the Cold War until it dissolved in 1991.

Aztec Empire
Ruled central Mexico from its island capital Tenochtitlan, one of the largest cities in the world at the time. It fell to Hernán Cortés and his Indigenous allies.

Incan Empire
The largest empire in pre-Columbian America, running along the Andes. It was linked by a vast road network and recorded information with knotted cords called quipu. It fell to Francisco Pizarro's conquistadors.

Mayan Civilization
A civilization of city-states in southern Mexico and Central America. It developed the only full writing system in the pre-Columbian Americas and advanced astronomy and mathematics, including zero. The last independent Maya city, Nojpetén, fell to the Spanish in 1697.

Ottoman Empire
Founded by Osman I in Anatolia. It took Constantinople in 1453, and at its peak under Suleiman the Magnificent it spanned three continents. It was dissolved after WWI.

Out of Africa Migration
Modern humans spread from Africa to the rest of the world. Nearly all non-Africans descend from this dispersal and carry a small amount of Neanderthal DNA from it.

Peopling of the Americas
Humans crossed from Siberia into the Americas, probably via the Bering land bridge or along the coast, and spread all the way to South America. Some evidence suggests even earlier arrivals.

Domestication of the Dog
Dogs were the first domesticated animal, descended from ancient wolves, and were tamed by hunter-gatherers long before farming. The exact timing and place are still debated.

Neolithic Revolution
The shift from hunting and gathering to farming and permanent villages, starting in the Fertile Crescent. It made cities, writing and states possible.

Development of agriculture
Farming arose independently in several places, including the Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica and the Andes, and spread across the world.

Black Death
A bubonic plague pandemic that killed roughly a third to half of Europe's population. It caused labor shortages that reshaped medieval society.

Australopithecus afarensis
An early hominin known from the famous "Lucy" skeleton. It walked upright but had a small, ape-sized brain.

Australopithecus africanus
A southern African australopithecine; the Taung Child skull was the first early human ancestor found in Africa.

Homo habilis
The "handy man", one of the earliest members of the genus Homo. It is associated with the first stone tools.

Homo erectus
The first hominin with near-modern body proportions and the first to leave Africa, spreading as far as Java. It probably used fire and made hand axes.

Homo heidelbergensis
A possible common ancestor of Neanderthals and modern humans, with a large brain. It hunted big game with wooden spears.

Homo neanderthalensis
Neanderthals were stocky, large-brained humans adapted to ice-age Europe and western Asia. They buried their dead and interbred with modern humans before going extinct.

Homo sapiens neanderthalensis
Neanderthals classified as a subspecies of our own species rather than a separate species. The name reflects the evidence that they interbred with modern humans.

Guerra de Independencia de México
Mexico's war of independence from Spain, sparked by Father Miguel Hidalgo's "Grito de Dolores" in 1810. It ended with Agustín de Iturbide's Army of the Three Guarantees taking Mexico City in 1821.

Guerra México-Estados Unidos
The Mexican-American War followed the US annexation of Texas. Mexico lost about half its territory, including California and the Southwest, in the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.

Guerra de Reforma
A civil war between liberals and conservatives over the 1857 constitution, which limited the power of the Church and the army. The liberals under Benito Juárez won.

Revolución Mexicana
A decade of civil war that overthrew the dictator Porfirio Díaz, with leaders like Madero, Villa and Zapata. It produced the 1917 constitution and land reform.

Benito Juárez
A Zapotec lawyer who became Mexico's first Indigenous president. He led the liberal Reform, and resisted the French intervention and Emperor Maximilian.

Porfirio Díaz
A general who ruled Mexico for most of 1876-1911 (the "Porfiriato"), bringing railroads and foreign investment but also dictatorship and inequality that led to the Revolution.

Lázaro Cárdenas
Mexico's most celebrated modern president. He nationalized the oil industry in 1938, creating Pemex, and carried out large-scale land reform.

Manuel Ávila Camacho
Brought Mexico into WWII on the Allied side, sending the Escuadrón 201 air squadron. He moderated Cárdenas's reforms.

Miguel Alemán Valdés
The first civilian president after the Revolution. He pushed industrialization and built projects like the Ciudad Universitaria of UNAM, but his administration was known for corruption.

Adolfo Ruiz Cortines
Known for austerity and fighting corruption. Women gained the right to vote in federal elections in 1953, during his term.

Adolfo López Mateos
Nationalized the electricity industry and expanded social security and free textbooks. He also won Mexico the bid for the 1968 Olympics.

Gustavo Díaz Ordaz
Presided over the 1968 Olympics, and over the Tlatelolco massacre of student protesters ten days before they began.

Luis Echeverría Álvarez
Pursued a populist, "Third World" foreign policy. His term included the "Dirty War" against leftist dissidents, and ended in a currency devaluation.

José López Portillo
Borrowed heavily against an oil boom, then nationalized the banks when oil prices fell and the 1982 debt crisis hit.

Miguel de la Madrid
Began Mexico's shift to free-market policies during the "lost decade" after the debt crisis. His government's slow response to the 1985 Mexico City earthquake was widely criticized.

Carlos Salinas de Gortari
Privatized state companies and signed NAFTA. His term ended in 1994 with the Zapatista uprising, political assassinations, and the peso crisis.

Ernesto Zedillo
Handled the 1994 peso crisis and carried out electoral reforms. In 2000 he accepted the PRI's first presidential defeat in 71 years.

Vicente Fox
The first opposition (PAN) president after 71 years of PRI rule, a milestone in Mexico's democratization.

Felipe Calderón
Declared war on the drug cartels using the military. Violence rose sharply during his term.

Enrique Peña Nieto
Returned the PRI to power and opened the energy sector to private investment. His term was hurt by corruption scandals and the disappearance of 43 students in Ayotzinapa.

Andrés Manuel López Obrador
"AMLO", a left-wing populist who founded the Morena party. He promised a "Fourth Transformation" of Mexico with anti-corruption and welfare programs.

Jomon Period
Japan's prehistoric hunter-gatherer culture, named for its cord-marked pottery, some of the oldest pottery in the world.

Yayoi Period
Wet-rice farming, bronze and iron arrived from the Asian mainland, and Japanese society became more hierarchical.

Nara Period
Japan's first permanent capital was at Nara, modeled on Tang China. The earliest Japanese histories, the Kojiki and Nihon Shoki, were written, and Buddhism flourished under state support.

Heian Period
The capital moved to Heian-kyō (Kyoto), and court culture flourished. It produced classics like The Tale of Genji. At the end, the rising samurai class fought the Genpei War.

Kamakura Period
The first shogunate: a military government ruled from Kamakura while the emperor reigned in Kyoto. It fought off two Mongol invasions with help from typhoons (the "kamikaze").

Muromachi Period
The Ashikaga shogunate ruled from Kyoto. The period saw Zen, the tea ceremony and Noh theater flourish, but ended in the civil wars of the Sengoku era.

Edo Period
The Tokugawa shogunate brought 250 years of peace and near-isolation from the outside world. Popular culture like kabuki and ukiyo-e prints flourished in Edo (Tokyo).

Meiji Restoration
Imperial rule was restored under Emperor Meiji, ending the shogunate. Japan modernized and industrialized rapidly, becoming a major world power.

Prophet Muhammad receives first revelation
According to Islamic tradition, the angel Gabriel appeared to Muhammad in the cave of Hira near Mecca. This marks the beginning of the Quran's revelation.

Hijra (migration) of Prophet Muhammad and his followers from Mecca to Medina
The early Muslims fled persecution in Mecca to Medina, where Muhammad led the first Muslim community. The Islamic calendar counts its years from the Hijra.

Battle of Badr, the first major battle between Muslims and non-Muslims
A small Muslim force defeated a larger army of the Quraysh of Mecca. The victory greatly boosted the new community's standing.

Conquest of Mecca by Muslim forces led by Prophet Muhammad
Mecca surrendered almost without a fight. Muhammad cleared the idols from the Kaaba, making it the center of Islamic worship.

Death of Prophet Muhammad
Muhammad died in Medina, having united most of Arabia. The question of his succession later led to the split between Sunni and Shia Islam.

The first caliph, Abu Bakr, is elected and begins the Ridda Wars
Abu Bakr, Muhammad's close companion and father-in-law, became the first caliph. The Ridda ("apostasy") Wars reunited Arabian tribes that broke away after Muhammad's death.

Umayyad dynasty is established with its capital in Damascus
After the First Fitna (civil war), Muawiya founded the first hereditary caliphate. Under the Umayyads the caliphate expanded from Spain to Central Asia.

Abbasid revolution leads to the overthrow of the Umayyad dynasty and the establishment of the Abbasid Caliphate with its capital in Baghdad
The Abbasids overthrew the Umayyads and moved the center of power east, founding Baghdad in 762. One Umayyad prince escaped to Spain and founded the Emirate of Córdoba.

The House of Wisdom is founded in Baghdad, which becomes a center of scholarship and learning
A center of the Islamic Golden Age, where scholars translated Greek, Persian and Indian works and advanced mathematics, astronomy and medicine. Al-Khwarizmi worked there.

The Crusaders capture Jerusalem, leading to a long period of conflict between Muslims and Christians
The First Crusade took Jerusalem and massacred many of its inhabitants. Saladin recaptured it in 1187.

Ottoman Empire is founded and becomes a major Muslim power
Osman I founded a small principality in northwest Anatolia that grew into the Ottoman Empire. Its sultans later claimed the caliphate.

The Mongols sack Baghdad, leading to the decline of the Abbasid Caliphate
Hulagu Khan's army destroyed Baghdad, killing the caliph and many of its inhabitants. According to legend, the Tigris ran black with the ink of the House of Wisdom's books.

Fall of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain
Ferdinand and Isabella took the Nasrid Emirate of Granada, completing the Christian Reconquista. The same year, they sponsored Columbus's first voyage.

The Islamic State of Iraq and Syria (ISIS) is established, leading to widespread conflict in the Middle East
A jihadist group declared a "caliphate" across parts of Iraq and Syria, known for brutal violence against civilians. A US-led coalition and local forces had destroyed its territorial hold by 2019.

First human settlement of Papua New Guinea
Modern humans crossed the sea to Sahul, the landmass that joined New Guinea and Australia at low sea levels. It was one of humanity's earliest sea crossings.

Arrival of Lapita people in the Bismarck Archipelago
The Lapita culture, known for its distinctive stamped pottery, appeared in the islands northeast of New Guinea. Its people were the ancestors of the Polynesians.

Lapita people settle in the Solomon Islands
Lapita voyagers spread through the Solomons, on the edge of "Near Oceania", the islands humans had reached tens of thousands of years earlier.

Lapita people settle in Vanuatu
Lapita settlers crossed into "Remote Oceania", islands no human had reached before. Vanuatu has one of the oldest Lapita cemeteries, at Teouma.

Lapita people settle in Fiji
Fiji was settled by Lapita voyagers crossing hundreds of kilometers of open ocean, the gateway to the islands further east.

Lapita people settle in Samoa
Lapita people reached Samoa. Polynesian culture developed there and in Tonga over the following centuries.

Lapita people settle in Tonga
Tonga was settled by Lapita voyagers from Fiji. Along with Samoa, it became part of the Polynesian homeland.

Lapita people settle in the Society Islands
An early date given here for settlement of the Society Islands (Tahiti's group). Modern radiocarbon studies place the first settlement much later, around 1000 CE.

Polynesians settle in the Cook Islands
Polynesian voyagers began spreading east from Samoa and Tonga into the Cook Islands. Current evidence suggests settlement may have come somewhat later, around 1000 CE.

Polynesians settle in the Hawaiian Islands
Polynesian navigators reached Hawaii, probably from the Marquesas or the Society Islands, using star navigation and double-hulled canoes.

Polynesians settle in Aotearoa (New Zealand)
The last major landmass on Earth to be settled by humans. The Polynesian settlers became the Māori, and hunted the giant moa to extinction.

Polynesians settle in Easter Island
Settlers reached remote Rapa Nui, where they later carved the famous moai statues.

Polynesians settle in the Society Islands (second wave)
Modern radiocarbon dating suggests this was when the Society Islands were really settled. They became a hub for the voyages that reached the far corners of East Polynesia.

Polynesians settle in Tahiti
Tahiti became one of the most important islands in Polynesia, a center of voyaging and religion.

Europeans begin exploring and colonizing the Pacific Islands
Magellan's expedition crossed the Pacific in 1521, reaching Guam and the Philippines. It was the start of centuries of European exploration and colonization of the islands.

French annexation of Tahiti
France made Tahiti a formal colony after decades as a protectorate. It is still part of French Polynesia today.

US annexation of Hawaii
After US businessmen overthrew Queen Liliʻuokalani in 1893, the US annexed Hawaii during the Spanish-American War. It became the 50th state in 1959.

Independence of Samoa
Western Samoa became independent from New Zealand, the first Pacific island nation to gain independence in the 20th century.

Independence of Tuvalu
The former Ellice Islands separated from the Gilbert Islands (now Kiribati) and became independent from Britain. It is one of the world's smallest countries.

Pythagoras
A Greek philosopher and mathematician who founded a mystical school that believed "all is number". The Pythagorean theorem is named after him, though it was known earlier.

Siddhartha Gautama
The Buddha, a prince from the foothills of the Himalayas who gave up his wealth to seek enlightenment. His teachings founded Buddhism. His dates are debated, and many scholars now place him about a century later.

Confucius
A Chinese philosopher who taught ethics, filial piety and good government. Confucianism shaped Chinese and East Asian society for over two thousand years.

Heraclitus
A Greek philosopher known for the idea that everything is in constant change: "you cannot step into the same river twice".

Socrates
The Athenian philosopher who questioned everything using the Socratic method. He wrote nothing himself, and was sentenced to death by drinking hemlock.

Plato
A student of Socrates who wrote philosophical dialogues like the Republic. He founded the Academy in Athens and developed the theory of Forms.

Aristotle
A student of Plato and tutor of Alexander the Great. He wrote on nearly every subject, from logic and biology to ethics and politics, and dominated Western thought for two thousand years.

Epicurus
Founded Epicureanism, which taught that happiness comes from simple pleasures, friendship and freedom from fear, including fear of death and the gods.

Zeno of Citium
Founded Stoicism in Athens, teaching virtue, reason and acceptance of what we cannot control. Stoicism later influenced Roman thinkers like Marcus Aurelius.

Euclid
A Greek mathematician in Alexandria whose Elements organized geometry from axioms. It was used as a textbook for over two thousand years.

Jesus
A Jewish preacher from Galilee who was crucified by the Romans. Christianity, the world's largest religion, is based on his life and teachings.

Muhammad
The prophet of Islam, born in Mecca. He united Arabia under the new faith, and his revelations form the Quran.

Al Khwarizmi
A Persian mathematician at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad. His book on "al-jabr" gave us the word algebra, and his name gave us "algorithm".

Da Vinci
The Renaissance polymath: painter of the Mona Lisa and The Last Supper, inventor, anatomist and engineer.

Copernicus
A Polish astronomer who proposed that the Earth orbits the Sun, launching the Scientific Revolution. His book was published the year he died.

Shakespeare
English playwright and poet, widely considered the greatest writer in the English language. He wrote Hamlet, Macbeth, Romeo and Juliet and over 150 sonnets.

Fermat
A French lawyer and amateur mathematician who helped found number theory and probability. His "Last Theorem" was scribbled in a margin and wasn't proven until 1994.

Newton
Formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation, and co-invented calculus. He also did foundational work in optics.

Bernoulli
Jacob Bernoulli, from a Swiss family of mathematicians. He discovered the constant e, proved the law of large numbers, and helped develop calculus and probability theory.

Bach
A German Baroque composer, a master of counterpoint. His works include the Brandenburg Concertos, the Well-Tempered Clavier and the Mass in B minor.

Euler
One of the most prolific mathematicians ever, with contributions across all of mathematics. He introduced much of modern notation, including f(x), e, i and π, and his identity links e, i and π.

Mozart
An Austrian child prodigy of the Classical era who wrote over 600 works, including The Magic Flute and his Requiem, before dying at 35.

Gauss
The "Prince of Mathematicians", who made fundamental contributions to number theory, statistics (the normal distribution), geometry and physics.

Augustin-Louis Cauchy
A French mathematician who put calculus on rigorous foundations and pioneered complex analysis.

Tesla
A Serbian-American inventor who developed the alternating current (AC) system and the induction motor. He died poor despite his many inventions.

David Hilbert
One of the most influential mathematicians of his time. His 23 problems set the agenda for 20th-century mathematics, and he tried to put all of math on formal axiomatic foundations.

Einstein
Developed special and general relativity and E = mc², and won the Nobel Prize for explaining the photoelectric effect.

Ramanujan
A self-taught Indian mathematician who produced astonishing results in number theory and infinite series. He worked with G.H. Hardy at Cambridge before dying at 32.

Haskell Curry
An American logician known for combinatory logic. The Haskell programming language and "currying" are named after him.

Alonso Church
A logician who invented the lambda calculus. Along with his student Turing, he showed that some mathematical problems can't be solved by any algorithm (the Church-Turing thesis).

Kurt Godel
An Austrian logician whose incompleteness theorems showed that any consistent formal system rich enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove.

Launch of Sputnik 1
The first artificial satellite, launched by the USSR. It shocked the US, started the Space Race, and led to the creation of NASA.

Mercury Program
The first US human spaceflight program. It flew six crewed missions, and John Glenn became the first American to orbit the Earth.

Vostok Program
The Soviet program that put the first human in space, Yuri Gagarin, and the first woman, Valentina Tereshkova.

Gemini Program
NASA's two-person program that tested spacewalks, rendezvous and docking, and long missions: everything needed to go to the Moon.

Lunar Orbiter Program
Five uncrewed NASA spacecraft that mapped the Moon's surface in detail to pick Apollo landing sites.

Apollo Program
NASA's program that landed humans on the Moon, fulfilling Kennedy's goal. Twelve astronauts walked on the Moon between 1969 and 1972.

Soyuz Program
The Soviet, and later Russian, crewed spacecraft program. Soyuz is still flying today, making it the longest-serving crewed spacecraft in history.

Skylab Program
America's first space station, built from a leftover Saturn V stage. Three crews lived aboard it; it fell to Earth in 1979.

Viking Program
Two NASA landers that became the first spacecraft to operate successfully on the surface of Mars, searching for signs of life.

Space Shuttle Program
NASA's reusable spaceplane, which launched Hubble and helped build the International Space Station. Two shuttles, Challenger and Columbia, were lost in disasters.

Vostok 1 (First Human Spaceflight)
Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space, orbiting the Earth once in 108 minutes.

Apollo Lunar Module (First Moon Landing)
Apollo 11: Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin landed on the Moon while Michael Collins orbited overhead. "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind."

Apollo-Soyuz Test Project (First Joint US-USSR Spaceflight)
An American Apollo and a Soviet Soyuz docked in orbit and their crews shook hands, a symbol of Cold War détente.

Hubble Space Telescope
A space telescope launched on the Space Shuttle. It needed a repair mission to fix a flawed mirror, then helped measure the age and expansion rate of the universe and took iconic deep-field images.

Chandra X-ray Observatory
NASA's flagship X-ray telescope, studying black holes, supernova remnants and galaxy clusters.

Gravity Probe B
A satellite experiment that used ultra-precise gyroscopes to test two predictions of general relativity: the geodetic effect and frame-dragging.

Kepler Space Telescope
A telescope that stared at one patch of sky to find exoplanets by the dimming of their stars. It found thousands, showing that planets are common in our galaxy.

James Webb Space Telescope
The largest space telescope ever launched, observing in infrared from beyond the Moon's orbit. It sees the earliest galaxies and studies exoplanet atmospheres.
